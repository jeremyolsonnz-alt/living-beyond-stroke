If It Doesn't Seem to Be Working

When It Feels Like Nothing's Happening

You've done the practice a few times. You don't feel different. Nothing obvious has changed, and a quiet voice is wondering whether this is pointless. This is one of the most common experiences with guided imagery — and it is not a sign of failure.

Here's the honest truth: guided imagery mostly doesn't feel like much while you're doing it. The work it does happens below the level of what you can feel — in networks of brain cells quietly being activated and strengthened. You can't sense that directly, any more than you can feel a muscle getting stronger during a single workout. So "I don't feel anything" is not evidence that nothing is happening.

Why you might not feel much

  • Imagining gets clearer with practice. Early sessions are often faint. That's the starting point, not the ceiling.
  • Tiredness, low mood, and pain all make imagining weaker. A flat session may say more about a hard day than about the practice.
  • Some people feel movement rather than see pictures — or barely experience either — and assume they're doing it wrong. They're usually not. They just imagine differently.

What actually helps

Keep going — gently

The benefit in the research comes from doing this regularly over weeks, not from any single powerful session. Little and often beats intense and rare. Sessions two through five usually feel more useful than the first.

Check how you imagine

The short imagery check shows whether you lean toward seeing or feeling — and if you're a "feeler," it tells you to swap every "picture" in the scripts for "feel." That one change makes the practice land for a lot of people who thought it wasn't working.

Watch, then imagine, then try

Watch the real movement first — a video, a family member's hand, your own good side in a mirror. Then imagine it. Then attempt it. That order works better than imagining cold, especially if your imagining is faint.

If it's still not clicking after a few weeks

That's worth a conversation, not a quiet giving-up. Revisit the imagery check, try the felt-sense wording, and talk to your physiotherapist or occupational therapist — many know motor imagery well and can help you adjust it. Guided imagery suits many people, but not everyone the same way, and that's alright.
