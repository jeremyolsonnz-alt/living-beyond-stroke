A Plain-Language Glossary

Words and Terms

Every term this site uses, explained in plain language. If a word anywhere on the site is unfamiliar, it should be here.

Grouped roughly by theme rather than alphabetically, so related ideas sit together. If something's missing, tell us on the contact page and we'll add it.

Imagining and the practices

Motor imagery
Imagining a movement without actually making it. Doing this switches on many of the same brain areas as the real movement, which is why it can help recovery.
Guided imagery
A structured imagining practice led by a script or a voice, rather than done freely. Every practice on this site is guided imagery.
Visual imagery ("seeing")
Imagining by picturing something in your mind — the shape of a hand, a room, a movement seen from outside.
Kinaesthetic imagery ("feeling")
Imagining by feeling a movement from the inside — the weight, effort, and position — without necessarily seeing anything. For recovery, this is the more important of the two.
Aphantasia
Having little or no ability to picture things in the mind, usually life-long. It's not caused by stroke and isn't a fault. People with aphantasia can still use the "feeling" side of imagery.
Action observation
Watching a movement being performed. It wakes up some of the same brain cells as doing the movement, and strengthens the imagining that follows.
The Ideal Being / the figure-merge
A step used in every practice here: you watch a figure perform a movement with ease, then the figure "steps into" you and you feel the movement from the inside. It's a way of moving from watching to doing.

The brain and body

Neuroplasticity
The brain's ability to change and rewire itself — to form new connections and shift jobs from damaged areas to healthy ones. It's the reason recovery after stroke is possible.
Motor cortex
The strip of brain that plans and sends out movement signals.
Corticospinal tract / internal capsule
The main "cables" that carry movement signals from the brain down into the spinal cord and out to the body. Strokes often disrupt this route.
Mirror neurons
Brain cells that fire both when you do a movement and when you watch someone else do it. They're part of why watching movement helps.
Hemiparesis
Weakness on one side of the body — a very common effect of stroke.
Spasticity
Muscle tightness or stiffness that can follow a stroke, sometimes making movement harder.
Proprioception
Your sense of where your body parts are without looking — how you can touch your nose with your eyes closed.
Vestibular system
The balance organs in your inner ear that sense head position and movement.
Cerebellum
The part at the back of the brain that coordinates balance and smooth movement.
Broca's area
A region, usually on the left, heavily involved in producing speech. Damage here can cause difficulty finding and forming words.
Aphasia / dysarthria
Aphasia is difficulty with language itself — finding words, understanding. Dysarthria is difficulty making the sounds because the speaking muscles are affected. A stroke can cause either or both.

Timing, rest, and research words

Dose
How much and how often you practise. In the studies, roughly 3–5 times a week is linked to results.
Adherence
Actually keeping up a practice over time. It matters more than intensity — a good practice done rarely does little.
Ultradian rhythm ("the 90-minute dip")
A natural cycle through the day where roughly every 90 minutes your brain dips into a brief, more restful, more open state — a good moment for a few breaths of imagery.
PETTLEP
A checklist for making imagined movement as close as possible to real movement (matching posture, timing, environment, and so on). It shapes how the scripts are written.
KVIQ
The Kinaesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire — the established tool clinicians use to measure how well someone imagines. The site's imagery check is inspired by it, but is not the KVIQ itself.
RCT / meta-analysis
An RCT (randomised controlled trial) is a careful study comparing a treatment against a control. A meta-analysis pools many studies together to see the overall picture. Both are strong forms of evidence.
