For the People Around the Person

For Family and Friends

If someone you care about has had a stroke, you are already part of their recovery — often more than you realise. Here's how to help with this programme, and how to avoid the common mistakes that come from caring too hard.

You don't need any medical training to help with this. The guided imagery programme is a set of gentle mental practices, and some of the most useful support is simple: your presence, your patience, and a few small things done well.

How you can genuinely help

Read the scripts aloud

Each practice has a written script. Read slowly — more slowly than feels natural — with long pauses. A calm, familiar voice is often better than any recording. If the person has trouble reading or concentrating, this may be the single most useful thing you can do.

Watch movements together

Seeing a movement done smoothly helps the brain rehearse it. Before a practice, you might slowly open and close your own hand, or take a few steps, for them to watch. It sounds almost too simple — but watching real movement genuinely strengthens the imagining that follows.

Protect their rest

Tiredness after a stroke is real and physical. When they need to rest, help make that possible — a quiet room, fewer visitors, permission to stop. Rest is when the brain does its repair work. Protecting it is protecting their recovery.

The kindest mistakes to avoid

  • Don't treat rest as giving up. After a stroke, resting is part of the work, not a break from it.
  • Don't do everything for them. Where it's safe, letting them attempt things — even slowly, even imperfectly — is part of how the brain relearns.
  • Don't measure progress day to day. Recovery moves in weeks and months, with ups and downs. Daily scorekeeping tends to discourage everyone.
  • Don't push the practice. It works best when it's wanted. Offer, don't insist.
Look after yourself too

Supporting someone through stroke recovery is demanding, and it can go on for a long time. You cannot pour from an empty cup. Taking breaks, accepting help, and keeping some of your own life going are not selfish — they are what make you able to keep showing up. If you're struggling, please reach out to your own doctor or a carers' support service.
