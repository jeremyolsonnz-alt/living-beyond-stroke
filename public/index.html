"What can the mind contribute to the body's work of recovery?"

I am Jeremy. I hold a Masters of Social Sciences majoring in Psychology from Waikato University, New Zealand. My graduate research — conducted at Deakin University in Melbourne under Professor Robert Cummins — examined what helps people not merely survive serious disability, but flourish. I also hold a Diploma of Solution-Oriented Hypnosis.

Over two decades of work in rehabilitation, imagery, and wellbeing, the question I keep returning to is this: what is the mind's role in recovery? Not in any mystical sense, but in the precise neurological sense. The brain is plastic. Mental practice activates real motor networks. These are not beliefs — they are findings, replicated across hundreds of studies.

This site brings together the research on guided imagery and stroke rehabilitation, and a free programme built from that evidence. Nothing here is a substitute for clinical care. What it offers is a complement — a practice you can carry into the spaces between therapy sessions, into the morning before you rise, and into the quiet before sleep.

Pillar Two · The Programme

Five Practices, One Method

Each practice follows the same three-phase structure — Ideal Being, Anatomical Pathway, Breath and Light — adapted to a specific target area. You can begin with whichever practice is most relevant to you.

Practice 01
Arm and Hand
Reaching, grasping, and fine motor movement. The strongest evidence base in the programme.
Practice 02
Walking and Gait
The full walking cycle, from weight shift to push-off. Imagery of movement through space.
Practice 03
Balance and Posture
Sitting upright, standing steady, trusting the body's capacity to find and hold its centre.
Practice 04
Speech and Language
Finding words, forming sounds, and speaking with ease. Framed honestly: weaker evidence than motor exercises.
Practice 05
Fatigue and Rest
Post-stroke fatigue is real and common. This practice supports genuine rest and energy restoration.

Pillar One · The Evidence

Why Mental Practice Works

When you vividly imagine a movement, your brain activates many of the same neural circuits it uses to actually perform that movement. This is not metaphor — it is measurable on fMRI. And it has implications for rehabilitation that are only beginning to be understood.

Neuroplasticity

The adult brain is not fixed. After stroke, the cortex reorganises — nearby areas take over functions of damaged tissue. Mental practice drives and accelerates this reorganisation.

Motor Imagery

Imagining movement activates the supplementary motor area, premotor cortex, and primary motor cortex — the same network engaged in physical movement. Repeated activation strengthens synaptic connections.

"Mental practice added to physical practice improves arm and hand function after stroke more than physical practice alone."

Braun et al. (2006) · Systematic Review · Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation

An Honest Note

Nothing on this site is a treatment, a cure, or a substitute for rehabilitation medicine. The guided imagery programme is a self-directed practice complement, offered freely on the basis of published motor imagery research. The three-phase integration used in these practices — while drawing on peer-reviewed science — has not been clinically tested in this exact form. Some people will find it helpful. Others will not. That is the honest position.

If you are currently under the care of a physiotherapist, occupational therapist, or speech-language pathologist, these practices are designed to work alongside that care — not to replace it. You may wish to share them with your therapist.
